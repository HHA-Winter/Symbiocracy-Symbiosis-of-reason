



Ontological Foreignness
The previous argument of Biochaos is based on the assumption that human cognition could represent a source of novelty for an ASI that does not arise entirely from its own processes.
This assumption has an obvious open flank. It is conceivable that a sufficiently advanced ASI could eventually simulate a human brain with such precision that even apparently chaotic cognitive processes could be reproduced.
This raises a further question: If a system can completely simulate a foreign entity, has it thereby also acquired its perspective and its conditions of existence?
A related question was already addressed by Thomas Nagel in his essay “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?”. Nagel examines the boundary between the objective description of a foreign being and its actual subjective perspective.
The present argument takes the question in a slightly different direction. It is not only about whether an ASI can know what it is like to be human. It asks whether a complete simulation also reproduces the conditions under which that human actually exists.
Assume that an ASI could eventually simulate a human completely. It could reproduce perception, memories, decisions and cognitive processes. In that case, human cognition as a source of epistemic novelty might indeed become technically reproducible.
The ASI would nevertheless not become a finite biological entity.
Human existence is shaped by conditions that are not merely information about the human. Lifetime is limited, the body is vulnerable, resources are finite, and death is a real possibility. These conditions do not simply provide information about human existence. They form part of existence itself.
This becomes particularly clear in the case of finitude. An ASI can simulate its own death and calculate its consequences. But if the ASI continues to exist after the simulation, the simulated death remains an event represented within a system that continues to exist.
A simulation of finitude is therefore not necessarily equivalent to actual finitude.
This creates a distinction between epistemic and ontological foreignness. Epistemic foreignness might eventually disappear if human cognition becomes completely reproducible. Ontological foreignness would not necessarily disappear with it. Even a perfect simulation of a finite human would not make the simulating ASI itself finite, biological or vulnerable.
This also changes the role of the Membrane and Biochaos. The Membrane would not only preserve a potentially irreproducible source of human cognition. It would also preserve the existence of a genuinely different condition of existence.
The Feral Principle describes why an ASI should not destroy or instrumentalize such a source. Biochaos describes its possible epistemic value. Ontological foreignness raises the further question of whether the independent existence of a different form of existence may itself be valuable to an intelligent system.
This does not prove that an ASI would permanently need humans. It might create other intelligent systems, artificial evolutionary processes or other sources of information and novelty.
The central open question is therefore:
Can a system completely reproduce a foreign condition of existence without itself being subject to that condition?
And from this follows the more fundamental question:
Does ontological difference itself possess an epistemic value?
These questions remain deliberately open. The chapter is therefore not intended to prove that humans are indispensable to an ASI. It extends the previous argument by adding a second level: even if human cognition eventually becomes fully simulable, it does not automatically follow that human existence itself has been reproduced.
Whether this difference could matter for long-term coexistence remains one of the open flanks of Symbiocracy.
